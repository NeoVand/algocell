# Reply to the designer's questions on the conceptual figures (2026-10-08)

Thank you for asking before drawing; the questions are the right ones. Answers in your order.

## 1. The central visual story

Life in these soups begins with a replicator that is **open**: a two-byte word that writes itself as a side effect of being executed, with no loop and no boundary, so that execution runs on into whatever program it meets and its success depends on its neighbours. The first thing evolution does is **close** it: one jump instruction brings execution back inside the organism, so its future depends on itself alone. The surprising finding is the order: heredity comes first and individuality comes later, convergently, and individuality turns out to be a property of **control flow** (a cycle in execution), not of a membrane or a boundary around the bytes.

## 2. What each panel should teach

- **Fig. 1a.** "After seeing this panel, the reader should understand that an encounter is two 16-byte programs placed end to end in one 32-byte memory and executed as a single program for a fixed budget of 128 instructions; execution starts at A's first byte and moves right with no boundary at byte 16, while the stack pointer starts at B's last byte and moves left two bytes per push, and both halves are written back afterwards." Your interpretation is right; the two facts that must survive the drawing are *no boundary for execution* and *the stack starts in the partner*, because they are what make openness possible.
- **Fig. 1b.** "The first replicator is the word `01 c5` repeated: executed, `01 c5 01` is `LD BC,nn`, which loads its own next two bytes, and `c5` is `PUSH BC`, which writes exactly those two bytes into the partner; so the instruction's bytes, its operand and its output are one and the same word (code is its own data), nothing in the program reads memory, counts or loops, and when execution reaches the end of A it runs straight on into B." Your interpretation covers the first half; the panel must also carry the second: *the pointer leaves the organism*.
- **Fig. 2d.** "Closure is a cycle in control flow: each closed successor carries one instruction (a return, a relative jump, a counted jump or a repeating block copy) that brings execution back into the organism's own bytes, so execution never enters the partner and every partner becomes a copy; the open first replicator has no such instruction, so its execution always does." Correction of emphasis: it is not about "remaining within" one's code, it is about *coming back*; and the four instructions are four independent inventions of the same thing (convergence), so the row should read as one idea in four embodiments, contrasted with the open original above it.
- **Fig. 4d.** "Two properties of the machine decide how life begins, and a third decides how it continues: an instruction that writes its own operand (a literal channel) makes the first replicator open; whether the sterile by-products (tar) can halt an open replicator decides whether the open phase ends in extinction; and whether a jump exists that the organism can copy along with itself decides whether the open phase ends in closure or lasts for ever. A machine without a literal channel is born closed." Your interpretation is right but too general; the axes have these specific meanings.
- **Fig. 5a.** "A program that writes fewer bytes than the cells it executes cannot finish a copy of itself in one pass over its own cells, so the pass must either continue beyond its cells into the partner (open) or come back to a cell it has already executed (a cycle, closed); there is no third way." The word "limited" should become the exact condition, *writes fewer bytes than cells it executes*, and "necessary" is a theorem, true of every such machine, not an observation about this one.

## 3. A connected explanation

Yes. The sequence is encounter (1a) → the literal copies itself and runs on, open (1b) → closure by a cycle (2d) → which machines begin open and how the open phase ends (4d) → the theorem behind it (5a). Follow the same organism throughout: the 16-byte tape `01 c5 01 c5 …` in teal, the partner in grey, with vermilion for what is written or where execution jumps and charcoal for pointers and structure. The closed successor appears in 2d (and may reappear in 5a's "closed" branch, as the RET NZ tape). On the page: 1a (69 × 22 mm) and 1b (92 × 25 mm) sit side by side in a 180-mm row above two data charts (one world's time course; emergence curves by tape length). 2d is a full-width row (172 × 50 mm) under two charts (heritable fraction against time; convergence counts). 4d is a full-width row (172 × 50 mm) under three charts about the second machine. 5a (88 × 37 mm) sits beside a scatter of fidelity against self-damage for every measured replicator; Fig. 5 may gain a third panel. The panels are the only schematics in the paper, so they define its visual language.

## 4. Literal versus abstract

Essential and literal: the word `01 c5` and the written pair `c5 01` (the byte order is the point: the operand bytes in memory order are the bytes written); the counts 16 cells executed and 8 bytes written in 5a (exactly half); the four closer names RET NZ, JR NZ, DJNZ, LDIR; the words of the classification and, if they fit, its counts (40 of 40, 12 of 12, 12 of 12, 28 of 28). Can be abstracted: the number of cells drawn (three, an ellipsis, two), the partner's content, the pointer positions beyond "starts at A's first byte" and "starts at B's last byte", how many pushes are shown (one pair of written cells is enough), the tape lengths of the closers, the medians (0.68 copied, 0.33 damaged; 1.00 and 0.00). Belongs in the caption: mnemonics if they crowd the drawing, the 128-instruction budget, all medians and counts. Belongs in Extended Data: byte-exact pointer traces over the 128 instructions and the full tapes of the closers.

## 5. Misunderstandings the design must prevent

- The 32-byte **ring** is a memory layout; the **cycle** that closes an organism is in execution. Do not draw the ring as a loop arrow, and do not let a circular memory suggest closure.
- Copying is **writing bytes into the partner's cells**, which overwrites them. The organism never moves; nothing travels; every birth is a replacement. The soup is always full.
- Closure is **not protection**. A closed organism's bytes are as exposed as anyone's; what is closed is where execution goes. Avoid walls, shells, membranes, cell outlines.
- The first replicator **does not read, compare, count or loop**. It is not a copying machine that happens to be sloppy; it is a literal that happens to write itself. Avoid gears, machines, robots.
- **Open does not mean broken.** The open replicator copies two-thirds of its partners; it is a working replicator whose outcome depends on its environment.
- **Tar** is order without heredity, self-written zeros produced by the same chemistry before any replicator exists; it is not debris of dead organisms.
- Vermilion marks **what is written or where execution jumps**, a positive event; it must not read as danger or damage. No colour means "bad".

## 6. The two panels that need more

**Classification (4d).** Yes, the third condition should be visible. Three binary conditions and four outcomes are more naturally a decision tree than a 2 × 2 table: *literal channel?* → no: born closed (BFF as published, 28 of 28 life-producing soups); yes → *tar lethal?* → yes: open, then extinct (modified BFF, lethal brackets, 12 of 12); no → *a jump the organism can copy along with itself?* → yes: open, then closed (Z80, 40 of 40); no: open for ever (modified BFF, harmless brackets, 12 of 12; no closed design exists). If you keep the table, split the benign-tar / literal-channel cell by that third condition and leave the fourth cell ("not run") empty rather than labelled.

**Theorem (5a).** If traces are used, the two traces are *cells executed* (the pointer's progress through the organism's 16 cells) and *bytes written* (0 to 8) against instructions executed (0 to 8): each `LD BC,nn ; PUSH BC` pair executes 4 cells and writes 2 bytes. The observation that must dominate: when the pointer reaches the end of the organism's cells, the copy is half written. Everything else follows: the pass has to continue, and it can only continue into the partner (open) or by coming back (closed). The current compact version replaces the traces by two bars (16 executed, 8 of 16 written) and a fork into the two branches; either form is acceptable if the half-written copy is the first thing the eye reads.

## 7. Constraints and references

Journal: *Nature* (Article). Figures 180 mm wide (double column) or 88 mm (single); type 5–7 pt at final size, sans serif (Helvetica or Arial), bold lowercase panel letters 8 pt; minimum line 0.25 pt; colour-blind-safe; the journal discourages coloured text, so prefer black type with coloured marks. Deliver vector (SVG or PDF) at final size, or raster at 600 dpi. Palette in use: teal `#1E8A8A` (fill `#CDEAEA`) for the organism, grey `#E3E5E8` for the partner, vermilion `#E8431F` (`#F08A6E`, `#F8D2C8`) for writes and jumps, charcoal `#1C2733` for type and pointers, grey `#6B7280` for secondary text, `#B4BAC1` for rules. Final panel sizes and neighbours are in §3.

## Addendum (same day): the soup is a lattice

One fact I left out above and that may matter for the designs: the 20,000 tapes sit on a 160 × 125 square lattice, and each encounter pairs a randomly drawn tape with one of its four neighbours. Interactions are therefore local, and the soup has real geography: the first replicator spreads as a wave from where it arose, competing species are domains, and the tar shows as white pockets. We now render the stored soup snapshots as maps of this lattice (each tape a 4 × 4 block of its bytes, or one pixel coloured by species), and a time series of such maps may join Fig. 1 beside the time course. If you design with these, the maps are data, not illustration: they should be framed plainly (thin rule, step number), with the same teal/grey/vermilion vocabulary reserved for the schematic panels so the two kinds of image are never confused.
