# Open replicators evolve closure in a digital primordial soup

*Nature Article draft v2, 2026-10-08. Title 61 characters. Summary ≤ 200 words. Subheadings ≤ 40 characters. Main text target ≤ 3,500 words. Every number is from a generated table named in the Methods (Data availability) and in `manuscript/NUMBERS_INDEX.md`; `[fill]` marks the two follow-up cells still running.*

---

Theories of the origin of life disagree about what came first but agree that at some point a self-propagating pattern became an individual, something whose persistence depends on itself rather than on its surroundings^1–4^. That step has never been watched. Soups of random computer programs interacting on a shared memory produce self-replicators without any fitness function^5–7^ and so offer a system in which the step can be observed, repeated and taken apart. Here we show, in thousands of worlds of random Z80 machine code, that heredity precedes individuality. The first replicator is a two-byte literal that writes itself, open to its surroundings and dependent on them; closure, a control-flow cycle that keeps the organism's execution inside its own bytes, evolves afterwards under the damage the open form suffers, and independent worlds converge on byte-identical closed genomes. We prove that closure requires such a cycle and that a machine's smallest replicator is open exactly when its instruction set can write a literal. The Brainfuck-family soup of Agüera y Arcas et al.^5^ is born closed as predicted; one added literal-writing instruction makes it start open, and shows that the open phase is a window whose length the chemistry of the tar decides. Individuality here is a property of a program's control, not a boundary in space.

## Main

Life is often defined by a list — metabolism, compartment, heredity — and origin-of-life research by which item came first^8–11^. A different tradition asks what makes a living thing an *individual*: Maturana and Varela's operational closure^1^, Rosen's closure to efficient causation^2^, Fontana and Buss's organizational closure^3^, and the information-theoretic individuality of Krakauer and colleagues^4^ all locate life in a system's dependence on itself rather than on its environment. Neither tradition has had an experimental system in which the transition from pattern to individual can be watched, replicated and dissected.

Soups of random programs offer one. Agüera y Arcas et al. showed that random Brainfuck-like programs executing on a shared tape give rise to self-replicators without any fitness function^5^; the Coreworld^6^ and random Lisp programs^7^ showed the same earlier, and random Z80 programs have recently been used to study the coevolution of replication and function^12^. These systems have nothing to protect — no energy, no membrane, no space, no explicit selection — so whatever becomes an individual there does so by the logic of self-reference alone. We built such a soup from a real instruction set, the Zilog Z80, on the GPU, with an assay that measures heredity by intervention rather than inspection, and ran it as an experiment: thirteen instruction-set ablations, tape lengths from 3 to 100 bytes, execution budgets from 32 to 512 steps per encounter, mutation rates over two decades, and pre-registered confirmatory stages with kill criteria.

### A soup with nothing to select for

The soup holds 20,000 programs ("tapes") of L bytes, randomly initialised (Fig. 1a). At each step 8,192 random pairs are drawn, of which about 3,980 are active; each pair is concatenated into a 2L-byte ring, executed by a Z80 core for 128 instructions starting at the first byte of the first tape with the stack pointer at the last byte of the second, and written back. A background mutation replaces a fixed number of random bytes per step. There is no fitness function and no replacement: a tape changes only by being executed or written into. The sweeps ran on 50 parallel GPUs (Methods).

Replication is measured by a *culture test* (Fig. 1b): a tape is executed against 64 random partners; the fraction of partners that become ≥ 75% copies of it is its copy success, and the tape is *heritable* if its offspring, run against fresh partners, produce copies in turn (gen2 ≥ 0.3). The test matters because the most common byte pattern in a soup is often not a replicator: a program may write an inert pattern into its neighbours, and a return-address smear can occupy 0.5% of the soup without inheriting anything. We date emergence by the first heritable exemplar among the three most common tapes with a share of at least 0.5%, and follow the heritable fraction of random cells as the population measure.

### Tar, then an open replicator

In every unablated world the same sequence follows (Fig. 1c,d). First comes *tar*: at the moment the first pattern reaches 10% occupancy, 24–34% of all bytes are zero at every tape length. The zeros are self-written — `PUSH`, `CALL` and `RST` with zero registers and return addresses write zeros wherever the stack pointer points, and zero is a no-op — compressible, spreading and sterile: its carriers have gen2 ≈ 0. It is the digital analogue of Benner's tar^13^, order without heredity made by the same chemistry that will make life.

Then the first heritable replicator appears, and it is the same program almost everywhere: the two-byte word `01 c5`, `LD BC,nn ; PUSH BC`, tiled across the tape. In the pre-registered confirmatory stage it is the modal first tape in 19/20, 12/20, 14/20 and 18/20 worlds at L = 16, 20, 50 and 64, the rest being its register variants, at a Kaplan–Meier median of 450–750 steps regardless of L (Fig. 1d). Its mechanism is a tautology (Fig. 1b): the literal `01 c5` is both the instruction `LD BC,$c501` and the data it loads; `PUSH BC` writes those two bytes onto the stack, which lies in the partner, and the pointer advances to the next copy of the same literal. Code equals data equals literal. It needs no loop, because the ring brings the pointer back; no counter, because every byte of the tape is the same word; and no test of its surroundings, because it never reads them. It is also nearly the only thing of its size that can replicate: of all 65,536 two-byte words tiled to 16 bytes, exactly five leave an all-zero partner holding a copy, all load–push pairs (`01 c5`, `11 d5`, `21 e5`, and the memory-load variants `2a e5`/`e5 2a`), and none copies into more than 81% of random partners. A further 246 words pass the heredity threshold without producing a single 75% copy — `CALL` instructions smearing return addresses — which is why we require faithfulness as well as heredity before calling anything a replicator.

### Closure evolves without a membrane

The pusher does not stay. It is *open*: executed against 256 random partners it copies into a median 0.66–0.73 of them (range 0.56–0.84 over 80 worlds and four lengths) and loses a quarter or more of its own bytes in a median 0.06–0.43 of encounters (Fig. 2a,b). The failures have one cause. After writing its copy the pointer runs on into the partner and executes the partner's bytes, which can poison the register pair before the next push. Against all-zero partners the pusher copies perfectly; its success falls as the density of active bytes in the partner rises, and no execution budget rescues it.

By 300,000 steps the dominant tape at L = 16 carries a control-flow instruction in 20/20 worlds and 17 of them end on the identical sequence `ad e3 21 e3 21 c0 ad c0` — `XOR L ; EX (SP),HL ; LD HL,nn ; RET NZ` — whose `RET NZ` returns the pointer into the organism's own body; at L = 50 all 20 worlds carry a `JR NZ` inserted into the pusher tiling, 14 of them byte-identical (Fig. 2e). Across the 233 unablated worlds of the exploratory stages, 92 gained a control-flow instruction between the first replicator and the final dominant and 3 lost one (exact McNemar P = 7 × 10⁻²⁴). Every such successor copies into 1.00 of 256 random partners with 0.00 self-damage, whatever instruction implements the cycle: `RET NZ`, `JR NZ`, `DJNZ`, `JP`, `JP (HL)`, `RET`, or the hardware loops `LDIR` and `LDDR` (Fig. 2a,b). The population tells the same story (Fig. 2c): at L = 16 the heritable fraction of random cells stays between 0.07 and 0.19 for the first 20,000 steps after emergence — the pusher's reign is a world of damaged copies — and rises to 0.75–0.94 when the closed successor takes over.

We call this property *closure*: the organism's bytes are a forward-invariant set under execution for every context (Methods). It is Maturana and Varela's operational closure made countable, and Krakauer's individuality made measurable by intervention: the closed organism's future depends on its own past alone. Note what closure is not. It is not a membrane; the closed organism's bytes are as exposed to writes as before. It is not a test of the environment; `RET NZ` tests a flag the organism set itself. It is a cycle in the organism's control flow that never leaves the organism (Fig. 2d). Closure takes longer the larger the organism: at L = 20, 19/20 worlds are closed by one million steps, at L = 64 only 8/20, with 12 still dominated by the open pusher, which there is also the least damaged open form in the atlas (self-damage 0.06) and so under the weakest pressure to close. The pre-registered expectation of ≥ 10/20 closures at both lengths was met at L = 20 only behaviourally — that family closes with `LDIR`, which our syntactic criterion did not count — and not at L = 64 (Methods, Pre-registration).

### Three load-bearing instructions

Which instructions does this need? The seed-paired ablation atlas (Fig. 3a) finds three load-bearing parts and nothing else. Removing *immediate loads* leaves 2/10 worlds alive at 300,000 steps; removing *stack writers* delays emergence 160- to 992-fold in median and hands the soup to a replicator built on the block-copy instruction `LDIR`; removing *all copying* (loads, pushes and block copy, 112 opcodes) or leaving only read-modify-write instructions produces no replicator in 40 worlds at one million steps. Every other ablation — block copy, calls and returns, memory loads, I/O, exchanges — is within seed-paired noise of the unablated soup. The mutation rate chooses the family that holds the soup, and the execution budget decides whether an organism can be faithful: at L = 100 the `LDIR` regime is faithful in 1/10 worlds at 128 steps per encounter and 10/10 at 256, while at 64 steps no exemplar ever reaches 0.5% of the soup yet 6/10 final soups are heritable populations with no clone — heredity carried by partial copies, the communal regime Woese placed before the Darwinian threshold^14^.

At L = 9 removing the stack writers makes life *more* likely (12/20 vs 6/20 worlds at 128 steps and 19/20 vs 5/20 at 512, Cochran–Mantel–Haenszel P = 3.9 × 10⁻⁶; Fig. 3d), because the tar they produce destroys nascent `LDIR` copiers before they assemble: an `LDIR` tape reaches the ten most common tapes in 5/20 worlds at a median 118,500 steps with stack writers present, against 20/20 within 3,400 steps without, and programs are destroyed 6.8 against 1.3 times per 1,000 interactions. Tar is not a nursery; it is a hazard that only an open literal-writer, which cannot be poisoned by zeros, gets through.

### No size floor, a geometry switch

Emergence time is flat from L = 16 to L = 100 (Fig. 1d): the pusher costs two bytes however long the tape, because tiling makes a large organism of a small word. Below 16 there is no monotone floor but a dead zone (Fig. 3b): life at L = 5 (whole-tape copiers) and L = 8 and from L = 13 up, none at L = 3, 4 and 7, and 4/10, 4/10 and 6/10 worlds at L = 9, 10 and 12, exactly where the pusher's own isolated heritability dips to 0.20–0.32. The dead zone is geometric: the stack pointer descends two bytes per push while the instruction pointer ascends, and on a 2L-byte ring they race; when a push lands on the literal the pointer is about to read, the register pair is poisoned. Padding the pair memory to a longer ring without changing the organism moves the collision and switches the dead zone off (Fig. 3c): at L = 12, rings of 28 or 36 bytes give 10/10 worlds alive at 350–600 steps where the native 24-byte ring gives 6/10 at a median 245,500. Closure, too, has a geometric channel: the `RET NZ` closer returns to an absolute address and copies 0.00 of partners on any ring but its own, whereas a relative-jump closer keeps control closed on every ring. Control can be made coordinate-free; this machine's reproductive channel cannot.

### Tar fools compression detectors

The transition of Agüera y Arcas et al. is detected by a drop in high-order entropy, byte entropy minus compressed size^5^. In our unablated soups that detector is anti-correlated with life at L = 25, 49, 64 and 81 (area under the curve 0.07–0.34 against the culture-test ground truth), because the zero flood compresses better than the replicators that replace it; the fraction of non-unique tapes and the raw bits per byte are robust (≥ 0.84 at every length but one), and occupancy of the dominant genotype is a poor proxy for a heritable population (0.61–0.95 by length). Tar is more orderly than life, and a one-byte organism, as we show below, is as compressible as tar. Any agnostic biosignature that scores order without testing propagation will be fooled by the chemistry that makes life possible.

### A literal channel starts life open

Why is the first replicator open, and must it be? Two counting arguments answer (Methods, Theorems). A straight-line program's pointer runs on into its partner, so a straight-line replicator is open by construction; and a closed replicator must execute more bytes of code than it has, because each byte of code writes fewer than one byte, so its pointer must revisit an address: closure requires a cycle. A straight-line replicator exists only if the instruction set can write a *literal* — content independent of the memory being overwritten — at a self-advancing pointer. The Z80 can (`LD rr,nn ; PUSH rr`). BFF, the Brainfuck family of Agüera y Arcas et al., cannot: `+` and `−` increment in place, `.` and `,` copy from memory, and a one-pass execution of 128 instructions cannot copy a 64-byte tape with three or more distinct bytes (it needs 64 writes, 63 head moves and 2 source changes). An exhaustive search over 16.4 million structured straight-line BFF programs under a wrapping pointer found only one-symbol fills, the BFF analogue of tar.

Sixty soups of 2¹⁷ programs run for 16,384 epochs test the consequences (Fig. 4). In BFF as published, 9 of 24 worlds produced a heritable replicator; every first replicator contains a loop (9/9), none is open (0/9), and seven of nine are closed by our strict criterion from the first sample in which they appear. A wrapping pointer produces more transitions (19/24; Fisher P = 0.008) but the same birth: 19/19 loop-bearing, 0/19 open, 16/19 closed. Adding one instruction that writes its own two operand bytes below the write head — the literal channel — changes the beginning completely: in 12/12 worlds the first replicator is the one-byte tiling of that instruction, whose operands are two more copies of itself, open (its pointer enters the partner in 100% of encounters), loop-free, and present at the first sample, epoch 64, with 53–60% of the soup. It is the `01 c5` of BFF. And then it dies: within about 200 epochs each of the 12 soups turns into tar that halts a pointer within a few dozen steps (an unmatched `]` ends BFF execution), copies into such partners are cut short and sterile, and the open replicator persists only as a 0.2% minority, heritable in isolation and unable to spread. No closed class evolves, and none can: no closed heritable replicator exists among 1.3 million periodic programs of this language up to period 10 (Methods). The open phase is therefore a *window*, not a stage. ### Closure as a theorem

The architectural statement holds for any sequential shared-memory machine whose pointer runs on into adjacent memory by default and whose code writes less than one byte per byte executed (Methods, Theorem 6): every closed replicator contains an executed cycle; a self-consistent literal-write instruction yields a straight-line, hence open, replicator the size of the literal; and so the smallest open replicator is strictly smaller than the smallest closed one and is present in a random soup with exponentially higher probability. In information terms (Lemma 7) closure is zero inflow of information from context to offspring, I(o; e | x) = 0, the limit that perfect fidelity across contexts requires; selection for fidelity is selection against listening to the environment. What the theorems do not decide is the dynamics after the open beginning: closure arrives with probability ≈ 1 − exp(−q∫n dt), where ∫n dt is the integral of the open population over time and q the rate of closing mutations. Benign tar (Z80 zeros are no-ops) lets the integral grow until closure is found; lethal tar (BFF brackets halt the pointer) closes the window first (Fig. 5).

### Discussion

Three claims rest on these results. First, in soups of random machine code heredity precedes individuality: the first replicator is a self-writing literal that is open to its surroundings and depends on them, and closure is an evolved refinement selected by the damage the open form suffers. Second, closure is topological rather than geometric: a cycle in control flow, not a boundary in space, reached by different instructions in different worlds that converge on the same function and often the same bytes. Third, the elements a substrate must offer are few and specific — a literal that is its own code, a write channel aimed at a neighbour, and a cycle — and the stages they produce are the ones origin-of-life theory has argued over in the abstract: Benner's tar^13^, Eigen's error threshold^15,16^ (which we observe as a curve in the `LDIR` regime), Woese's communal heredity before vertical descent^14^, the prelife of Nowak and Ohtsuki^17^, and Fontana and Buss's organizational closure^3^, here arising from a copier rather than in its absence.

Whether anything of this transfers to chemistry is open. What the system offers is a definition of closure that can be checked by intervention — execute the candidate in many contexts and ask whether its own bytes survive — and a warning for life detection: the first ordered, spreading, compressible thing in these worlds is not alive, and the first living thing is a two-byte tautology that no inspection of structure would flag^18,19^.

## Methods

**Soup.** 20,000 tapes of L bytes on a GPU; 8,192 pairs drawn per step, ≈ 3,980 active (0.395 active interactions per cell per step); pair memory a ring of 2L bytes (padded to P ≥ 2L in Stage F, padding never written back); execution by the Zilion Z80 core (`@neovand/zilion` 0.2.0, WGSL) for 128 instructions per encounter (32–512 in budget arms) from byte 0 with the stack pointer at the last byte of the partner; mutation 8,192/2^k random bytes per step (k = 4 unless stated). Thirteen instruction-set ablations replace opcode families by no-ops through a prefix-aware fetch hook (Extended Data Table 2). The emulator was validated against an independent Z80 reference with zero divergences in randomised 5,000-pair tests; it is not treated as ground truth for the Z80 itself.

**Culture test and partner tests.** A tape runs as the first program against 64 uniformly random partners for the condition's budget; copy success is the fraction of partners that become ≥ 75% copies at the best cyclic shift; gen2 is the copy success of the produced copies against fresh partners, weighted by the copy fraction; heritable means gen2 ≥ 0.3, faithful additionally ≥ 50% of partners copied. Partner-independence tests use 256 random partners and record self-damage (≥ 25% of the organism's bytes changed in one encounter). In BFF the culture test also records whether the instruction pointer entered the partner in each encounter; closed means ≤ 5% of encounters entered and ≥ 95% of partners copied, open means ≥ 50% entered.

**Events and statistics.** `t_rep`: first sample at which a top-three exemplar with share ≥ 0.5% is heritable (a clonality criterion by design); `t_faith` and `tq_10` (quasispecies occupancy ≥ 10%) recorded alongside; in BFF, where replicator populations are quasispecies, the exemplar is the most common class at the first sample where it is heritable and the population event is a heritable fraction ≥ 0.5 of 32 random tapes, with `t_rep` reported alongside. Kaplan–Meier medians with censoring at the horizon; Wilson intervals; Fisher's exact and Cochran–Mantel–Haenszel tests; seed-paired sign tests for ablation effects; exact McNemar for the paired gain of control flow; Mann–Whitney on censored times. Same-seed Z80 runs diverge (GPU scheduling), so every Z80 claim is a population claim over seeds; the BFF soup is bitwise reproducible across machines (identical epoch statistics on an Apple GPU and an NVIDIA L40S for six seeds over 16,384 epochs).

**Definitions and theorems.** A *self-writer* is a tape fixed under its own execution in some context. *Closure*: the organism's bytes are forward-invariant under execution for every context in the class of uniformly random partners. Theorem 1: no straight-line execution in one-pass BFF copies a 64-byte tape with three or more distinct byte values (writes + writer-head moves + source changes exceed 128). Theorem 2: if bytes written per byte of code executed is below one, a closed full replicator executes some address twice. Theorem 6: for any machine in which the pointer runs on into adjacent memory absent a control transfer and Theorem 2's bandwidth condition holds, a self-consistent literal-write instruction implies a straight-line (open) replicator the size of the literal, and its absence implies every replicator is loop-bearing. Lemma 7: I(o; e | x) > 0 implies imperfect fidelity across contexts; perfect fidelity implies I = 0. Proposition 3 (computer-assisted): the two-byte fixed points of the Z80 against the zero partner are exactly the five load–push words. Full statements and proofs in Supplementary Information.

**BFF.** cubff default semantics verified from source: two 64-byte tapes concatenated, pointer and heads at byte 0, ten instructions, every other byte a no-op, unmatched brackets halt, 2¹³ steps per encounter, 2¹⁷ programs, random ordered pairs each epoch, mutation 2⁻¹² per byte per epoch. Switches: `ip_wrap` (pointer wraps modulo 128); `literal` (byte 0x50 = `P a b`: write a at head1−1 and b at head1−2, then head1 −= 2, skip the operands); `nohalt` (unmatched brackets are no-ops). Our GPU kernel reproduces cubff's validation replicator and matches a CPU reference on randomised tests. 24 seeds each for the published and wrapping variants, 12 for each literal variant; events every 64 epochs; high-order entropy with brotli quality 2 as in ref. 5.

**Searches.** Two-byte census: all 65,536 words tiled to 16 bytes, culture test (32 partners) and partner test (256), plus the deterministic fixed-point test against the zero partner. BFF straight-line search: 16,447,860 programs (prefix ≤ 3 instructions + tiling unit of period 2–5 with a copy instruction), 2 random partners, wrapping pointer, alphabet prefilter then exact similarity, full culture test for survivors. Closed-replicator search: all 1,309,528 periodic tilings of {`P`, `[`, `]`, no-op} up to period 10 under the literal, wrapping, no-halt rules, three partners each.

**Pre-registration.** Stages D, F and G and the BFF cells were pre-registered with predictions and kill criteria before launch; every amendment is dated in the change log. Misses reported: the heritable-fraction thresholds of G1(c) failed at L = 50; the syntactic loop criterion of G2 omitted hardware loops (6/20 and 3/20 syntactic against 19/20 and 8/20 behavioural); the mechanism first claimed for an open BFF replicator under a wrapping pointer was falsified by the harness test before any soup ran and the prediction restated with both readings; a held prediction for the non-wrapping literal cell was corrected by the counting argument before it was run.

**Compute, code and data.** 3,560 Z80 runs (Stages A–G) and 84 BFF soups on Modal L40S plus local runs; ≈ $400 at list price. All code, conditions, raw summaries, generated tables and figure scripts are in the repository; each figure is produced from the generated tables by one script.
